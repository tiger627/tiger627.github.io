Last week I read an article by a Chinese AI writer. Well-structured, referenced Kahneman, laid out six design principles for AI systems. Smart stuff. And yet I kept feeling vaguely lost — not sure if the gap was my knowledge or his writing.

Turns out, it was neither.

## He's writing for someone I'm not

When I looked up his background, it clicked: former middle management in the IT department of a large organization, recently jumped to a CIO role at another.

He doesn't write to help practitioners build things. He writes for the IT manager who needs to walk into a board meeting next Monday and explain why the AI initiative should prioritize consistency over accuracy — with Kahneman's name behind it.

His habit of reading "100 papers in two weeks" isn't about mastery. It's a credibility tactic in a new role, at a new organization, where he needs to establish authority fast. The content is real. The volume is performance.

Once I understood his context, the article made perfect sense. And I stopped feeling inadequate for not reading 100 papers a week.

## Same content, completely different use

As a data and AI consultant working with Dutch SMEs, I don't need theoretical frameworks to justify decisions upward. I need insights I can use when a client asks: *"We have 15 source systems and nobody agrees on what 'customer' means — where do we start?"*

The Kahneman framework from that article — prioritize consistency before accuracy — works perfectly as a diagnostic question for that client. Not as a slide in a board deck. As a first conversation.

Same content. Different extraction.

## Why I struggle to write

This also helped me understand why I find it hard to produce content of my own.

I kept assuming the problem was insufficient input. Read more, then write more.

But that was never the issue. I have eight years of BI and data work across two countries. I have first-hand observations of how differently Dutch and Chinese companies approach AI. I have real conversations with clients about real problems, every week.

What I was missing was a simple habit: asking *"what's the principle behind what I just observed?"*

Experience and insight are not the same thing.

**Experience:** *"I've worked on data integration projects for mid-size enterprises."*

**Insight:** *"The hardest part of enterprise data architecture is never the technology — it's getting five departments to agree on a single definition of 'customer'."*

I had the experience. I just hadn't been converting it.

## One more friction: language

Writing in English adds another layer. Not because I can't — but because thinking in a second language consumes cognitive energy that would otherwise go into the ideas themselves.

My practical fix: think and draft in Chinese first, where ideas flow naturally. Then adapt — not translate — for an English audience. The Chinese version goes deeper on framework. The English version leads with the scenario. This is why I'm building a bilingual site: not just to reach two audiences, but because the workflow itself produces better content in both languages.

## The question worth asking

Before asking *"is this good content?"* — ask *"who is this written for?"*

A framework that helps a CIO justify AI investment might be useless to a consultant solving a client's data problem. The same content can serve both — but only if you're clear about what you're extracting from it.

For me, the Kahneman noise framework isn't boardroom ammunition. It's a question I can now ask every client:

*"Before we talk about whether your AI system is accurate — how consistent is it?"*

That one question is worth more than 100 papers I half-read.
